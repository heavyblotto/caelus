# First and last visibility

A star or planet near the Sun is lost in the glare for days or months. The
morning it is first seen again before sunrise is its heliacal rising, and the
evening it is last seen after sunset is its heliacal setting. Ancient records
report these days more often than they report positions, and the day depends
on where the observer stood. `heliacal` computes it for one place. The
[First Visibility instrument](/instruments/first-visibility) runs the same
search in the browser, for a year at a time.

```ts run
import { Engine, heliacal, julianDay, calendarDate } from "caelus";
import { embeddedData } from "caelus/data-embedded";

const engine = new Engine(embeddedData);

// The heliacal rising of Sirius at Memphis (29.85 N, 31.25 E) in 139 CE.
const start = julianDay(139, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, "julian");
const rising = heliacal(engine, "Sirius", start, 29.85, 31.25);

calendarDate(rising.jd, "julian");        // 19 July 139, 02:34 UT
calendarDate(rising.earliest, "julian");  // 18 July under a clearer sky
calendarDate(rising.latest, "julian");    // 20 July under a hazier one
```

Censorinus, writing in 238 CE, implies that Sirius rose on the Egyptian New
Year in 139 CE. The manuscripts give 21 July, and the reading is usually
emended to 20 July. The model puts the rising at Memphis on 19 July, and
between 18 and 20 July across the range of skies it tries. The date moves
with latitude: the same call gives 20 July at Alexandria and 15 July at
Thebes.

## The events

`kind` selects the event. The default is `"morning_first"`.

| Kind | What it is | Which bodies |
|---|---|---|
| `morning_first` | First seen before sunrise: the heliacal rising | Planets and stars |
| `evening_last` | Last seen after sunset: the heliacal setting | Planets and stars |
| `evening_first` | First seen after sunset | Mercury, Venus, the Moon |
| `morning_last` | Last seen before sunrise | Mercury, Venus, the Moon |
| `acronychal_rising` | Last evening the body rises after dusk, near opposition | Mars to Saturn and stars, arcus method |
| `cosmical_setting` | First morning the body sets before dawn, near opposition | Mars to Saturn and stars, arcus method |

`"heliacal_rising"` and `"heliacal_setting"` are accepted as names for the
first two. The target is a planet id, `"moon"`, or a name from
`engine.starNames()`. Asking for an event a body does not have throws.

The search returns the next event at or after the start instant, or `null`
when none is found within about 830 days. A star that never sets at the
observer's latitude has no heliacal rising, and returns `null`.

## How a day is decided

For each morning or evening the model follows the body from the horizon up
and asks, minute by minute, whether the eye could pick it out:

- The sky's brightness at the body is the sum of the night sky, twilight, and
  moonlight. Twilight depends on how far the Sun is below the horizon and how
  far the body is from it.
- The body's light is dimmed by the air it passes through. Close to the
  horizon that path is long, and the loss is several magnitudes.
- The eye has a threshold: the faintest point it can see against a
  background of a given brightness.

The body is visible when its magnitude, after the dimming, is brighter than
the threshold. The event falls on the first or last day on which that is true
at some moment. The formulas are the ones B. E. Schaefer published for this
purpose ("To the Visual Limits", *Sky & Telescope*, May 1998; moonlight from
Krisciunas and Schaefer, *PASP* 103, 1991), implemented from the published
equations.

The result reports the moment of best visibility on that day and the
conditions at that moment:

```ts run
rising.margin;         // 0.26 magnitudes to spare
rising.altitude;       // 4.0 degrees above the horizon
rising.sunAltitude;    // -6.0: the Sun six degrees down
rising.arcusVisionis;  // 9.5: the Sun's depression as Sirius crossed the horizon
```

`visibility` answers the same question for one instant instead of searching
for a day. It returns the sky brightness at the body, the limiting magnitude
there, and the margin.

```ts run
import { visibility } from "caelus";

// Sirius from Cairo, 5 August 2025, 02:35 UT
visibility(engine, "Sirius", julianDay(2025, 8, 5, 2, 35), 30.05, 31.25);
```

## The atmosphere is an input

One number describes the air: `extinction`, the loss in magnitudes for light
that crosses one thickness of atmosphere, in the V band. The default is 0.25,
a clear sky at sea level. A high desert site is near 0.15, and a humid or
dusty one is 0.35 or more. Every result echoes the value it used.

Nobody knows the haze on a morning three thousand years ago, so each event is
computed three times: at the stated extinction, and at that value less and
plus `spread` (default 0.05). `earliest` and `latest` are the event under the
clearer and the hazier sky. For a bright star the window is two or three
days. For Mars, which is faint and slow near conjunction, it can be three
weeks.

```ts run
const hazy = heliacal(engine, "Sirius", start, 29.85, 31.25, {
  extinction: 0.35, spread: 0.1,
});

calendarDate(hazy.jd, "julian");  // 21 July
```

`altM` gives the observer's height in metres. It thins the Rayleigh part of
the extinction; the total you state is still the total.

## Mercury and Venus

The inner planets have four events in each synodic cycle. Around inferior
conjunction the planet leaves the evening sky and reappears in the morning
within days. Around superior conjunction it leaves the morning sky and
returns in the evening after weeks.

```ts run
const babylon = [32.54, 44.42];
const from = julianDay(2025, 1, 1);

["evening_last", "morning_first", "morning_last", "evening_first"].map((kind) => {
  const ev = heliacal(engine, "venus", from, ...babylon, { kind });
  const d = calendarDate(ev.jd);
  return `${kind}: ${d.year}-${d.month}-${d.day}`;
});
```

In 2025 Venus is last seen in the evening on 18 March and first seen in the
morning on 24 March, six days later. It is last seen in the morning on 1
December, and returns to the evening on 12 February 2026.

## The lunar crescent

The Moon is not a point, and the limiting-magnitude model does not apply to
it. Its first and last crescent use the test B. D. Yallop derived at the
Nautical Almanac Office from 295 recorded sightings (NAO Technical Note 69,
1997). The test scores an evening with one number, `q`, from the Moon's
altitude above the Sun and the width of the crescent, at the best time to
look.

| Zone | q above | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0.216 | Easily visible |
| B | -0.014 | Visible under perfect conditions |
| C | -0.160 | May need optical aid to find the crescent |
| D | -0.232 | Visible with optical aid only |
| E | -0.293 | Not visible with a telescope |
| F | | Below the Danjon limit |

`heliacal(engine, "moon", ...)` with `kind: "evening_first"` returns the
first evening in zone A or B. Its `latest` is the first evening in zone A,
which is the same evening or the next.

```ts run
const crescent = heliacal(engine, "moon", julianDay(2025, 3, 25), 21.42, 39.83, {
  kind: "evening_first",
});

calendarDate(crescent.jd);  // 30 March 2025, 16:07 UT, at Mecca
crescent.zone;              // "A"
crescent.lagMinutes;        // 72: the Moon sets 72 minutes after the Sun
```

`lunarCrescent` runs the test for one local day, so you can see why an
evening fails. On 29 March 2025 at the same place the Moon set seven minutes
after the Sun and the test gives zone F.

```ts run
import { lunarCrescent, localDay } from "caelus";

const day = localDay(julianDay(2025, 3, 29, 12), 39.83);
lunarCrescent(engine, day, 21.42, 39.83).zone;  // "F"
```

## The arcus visionis method

Before visibility models, the rule was geometric. A body is first seen on
the day the Sun is a fixed angle below the horizon at the moment the body
rises. That angle is the arcus visionis, and tables of it for each planet go
back to Ptolemy. `method: "arcus"` applies the rule with the angle you give.
The engine has no table of its own.

```ts run
const byRule = heliacal(engine, "Sirius", start, 29.85, 31.25, {
  method: "arcus", arcusVisionis: 9,
});

calendarDate(byRule.jd, "julian");  // 19 July 139
```

The two methods connect through `arcusVisionis` on a visibility result: it is
the angle the physical model implies for that event. Fed back to the arcus
method, it returns the same day.

The arcus method also covers the two events at opposition. The acronychal
rising is the last evening the body rises with the Sun at least the stated
angle down, and the cosmical setting is the first morning it sets so. The
visibility model does not offer these two, because "seen to rise" needs a
threshold altitude that no published model fixes.

## How it compares

The dates were compared with the heliacal functions of Swiss Ephemeris 2.10,
which carry a later and more detailed version of Schaefer's model. Both sides
were given the same extinction, 0.25, at five sites from Cape Town to London
and four epochs from 600 BCE to 2020. The stars were fed to both from the
same catalog rows.

| Group | Events | Same day | Within 1 day | Within 3 days | Largest difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ten bright stars | 198 | 68 | 183 | 198 | 3 days |
| Mars, Jupiter, Saturn | 58 | 17 | 37 | 55 | 6 days |
| Venus | 40 | 28 | 40 | 40 | 1 day |
| Mercury | 38 | 19 | 36 | 38 | 3 days |
| The Moon | 40 | 39 | 40 | 40 | 1 day |

In 372 of the 374 events the other answer falls inside the `earliest` to
`latest` window. The six-day case is an evening last of Mars, where the
planet fades slowly into twilight and the window itself is 22 days wide.
In four further cases the two models chose different apparitions, because
one judged a poor apparition of Mercury or Mars visible and the other did
not. `python/validate_heliacal.py` reproduces the table.

Two models of the same phenomenon agreeing is not a measurement of either
against the sky. Observed heliacal dates scatter by several days around any
model, from weather and from the observer. Treat the window, and not the
single date, as the answer.

## Through the MCP server

The `heliacal` tool takes a target, a latitude and longitude, and a start
year, and returns every event the target has, in date order, each with its
window. Years are astronomical and dates before 15 October 1582 are in the
Julian calendar, as in `date_sky`.

## Limits

- The model is for the naked eye. It has no term for a telescope or
  binoculars.
- The horizon is flat and at the observer's height. A ridge to the east
  delays a rising by the time the body takes to clear it.
- Extinction is one number for the whole sky and the whole season.
- Before about 800 BCE the uncertainty in the clock correction ΔT is 18
  minutes or more, and an hour at 3000 BCE. That shifts which longitude on
  Earth sees a given sunset, and can move a marginal crescent date by a day.
  Star and planet dates are not sensitive to it.
  [Deep Time](/docs/deep-time) has the figures.
