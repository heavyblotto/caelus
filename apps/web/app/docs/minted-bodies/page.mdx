# Minted bodies

The engine ships packs for Ceres, Pallas, Juno, Vesta, Pholus, and Chiron.
There are several hundred thousand other numbered asteroids, and bundling
them is not practical. A pack for any one of them can be minted on request:
fitted to JPL Horizons, measured, and published with a certificate that
states what was measured.

## Minting

`python/mint_body.py` takes the asteroid's IAU number.

```bash
python3 python/mint_body.py 433 --year0 1990 --year1 2030 --out minted/
```

It samples the body's heliocentric position from Horizons, fits a Chebyshev
pack, and writes the pack in the [binary form](/docs/data-tiers). It then
fetches the body's position at 300 further instants the fit never sampled and
measures the published bytes against them. The run fails if the error is over
its target, which is the smaller of 5e-6 AU and half an arcsecond at the
body's closest approach to the Earth in the span.

For 433 Eros over 1990 to 2030 the result is a 10.5 KB pack with an error of
8.4e-8 AU, or 12.6 km. Eros came within 0.179 AU of the Earth in January
2012, and at that distance the error is 0.10 arcseconds.

Two files come out: `eros.cpk`, the pack, and `eros.cert.json`, the
certificate.

| Certificate field | What it states |
|---|---|
| `id`, `number`, `name` | The engine id, the IAU number, and the name as Horizons gives it |
| `source.solution` | The orbit solution the samples came from, such as `JPL#659` |
| `span` | The years the pack serves |
| `error.max_au` | The largest distance between the pack and Horizons at the check instants |
| `error.min_geocentric_au` | The body's closest approach to the Earth in the span |
| `error.angle_bound_arcsec` | `max_au` seen from that closest approach: the most the pack can move a geocentric position |
| `pack.sha256`, `pack.bytes` | The hash and length of the pack the measurement was made on |

Horizons serves small bodies from 1600 to 2500, so a span cannot go outside
those years. The default is 1800 to 2200.

## Loading

`loadMintedBody` fetches the certificate and the pack, checks the pack's
length and SHA-256 against the certificate, and registers the body under the
certificate's id. A pack that does not match is refused before it is decoded.

```ts
import { Engine, loadMintedBody, julianDay } from "caelus";
import { embeddedData } from "caelus/data-embedded";

const engine = new Engine(embeddedData);
const base = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/heavyblotto/caelus/minted/";

const cert = await loadMintedBody(engine, "eros.cert.json",
  async (file) => (await fetch(base + file)).arrayBuffer());

engine.position(cert.id, julianDay(2012, 1, 31)).lon;
cert.error.angle_bound_arcsec;  // how far the pack can move that longitude
```

The engine does no I/O of its own. The third argument is your function for
fetching a file that sits beside the certificate, so the same call works in a
browser, in Node, and from a local directory. If you already hold the bytes,
`registerMintedBody(engine, bytes, certificate)` does the check and the
registration without the fetch.

Once registered, the body answers `position`, `longitude`, and
`heliocentric` like any other, and it throws outside the certificate's span.
`engineCapabilities` lists it with the source `"minted"` and that span, and
`engine.minted` holds the certificate.

## What the certificate does and does not cover

The certificate measures the pack: the body's heliocentric position against
the source it was fitted to. A position in a chart also depends on the
Earth's position and on the light-time, aberration, and nutation the engine
applies, and those have their own error. For Eros at its closest the whole
position, compared with the apparent place Horizons gives, differs by 0.27
arcseconds. Most of that is the Earth: an error of a few times 1e-7 AU in
the Earth's position is a fraction of an arcsecond at 0.18 AU and would be
several arcseconds for an asteroid passing ten times closer.

The orbit solution is the one Horizons held on the day of the mint. Orbits
are refined as observations arrive, so a re-mint years later can differ, by
more for a recently discovered body than for one observed since the
nineteenth century. The certificate records the solution so the difference
can be traced.

## The workflow

`.github/workflows/mint-body.yml` runs the mint on GitHub when dispatched
with a number and a span. It loads the result back through the Python
engine, publishes the pack and certificate on the repository's `minted`
branch, and rewrites `index.json` there, which lists every minted body with
its span and angle bound. The address in the example above is that branch.
The workflow has not been run yet, so the branch does not exist until the
first mint.

## Limits

- Numbered asteroids only. Comets and unnumbered objects are not minted.
- One span per body. Minting a body again replaces its files.
- The Python reference loads a minted body with
  `astroengine.minted.load_minted_body`, from files on disk.
